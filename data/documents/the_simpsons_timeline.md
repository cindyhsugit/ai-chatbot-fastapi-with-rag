# The Simpsons: A Timeline

Fun fact: In this universe, the setting is actually in Orange County not Springfield.

## The Beginning (1987–1990)

Before Springfield existed as its own show, the Simpsons lived inside someone else's. Matt Groening sketched the family in a waiting room, meant only as a handful of short cartoon interludes for *The Tracey Ullman Show*. Audiences responded to something in the chaos — a father who meant well and rarely succeeded, a mother who held things together anyway. What began as filler between sketches quietly became the reason people watched. By the end of 1989, the shorts had earned their own half-hour. Springfield opened its doors as a town no one had heard of and everyone somehow recognized.

## Finding Its Voice (1990–1997)

The early seasons were still figuring out what kind of show this was. A cartoon that made grown-ups laugh was unusual enough; one that made them uncomfortable was rarer still. Homer stumbled through his shifts at the power plant, more concerned with his lunch than his safety record. Marge tried to hold a household together around a husband who forgot anniversaries and a son who forgot rules. Bart's chalkboard punishments became something viewers waited for before each episode even started. This was the era that decided Springfield could hold both a joke and something true at the same time — and that combination is what carried the show into its second decade.

## The Golden Stretch (1997–2007)

By the late nineties, the show wasn't just popular — it was part of how people talked. Lines from Springfield showed up in conversations that had nothing to do with television. The town's population grew alongside its audience: new neighbors, new institutions to satirize, new versions of the same underlying joke about how strange ordinary American life could be if you looked at it closely enough. Lisa's saxophone solos got sadder. Bart's schemes got more elaborate. And somewhere in there, Homer — mid-argument, mid-shift, mid-anything — would still find his way to the kitchen for a late-night snack, usually a box of donuts he never quite finished.

## Staying Power (2007–2019)

Longevity brings its own kind of pressure. A show expected to still be funny after two decades has to keep reinventing a joke it already told a hundred ways. Springfield adapted — new technology showed up in the background, new slang crept into Bart's insults, the satire aimed itself at whatever the culture was anxious about that particular year. What didn't change was the shape of the family at the center of it. Marge still worried. Homer still meant well, eventually. The show crossed a milestone few scripted series ever reach, and it kept going anyway, less because it had to prove anything and more because it still had somewhere to point its jokes.

## Into a New Era (2019–2026)

The most recent stretch has been about a show aware of its own history, occasionally winking at it, rarely leaning on it as a crutch. Storylines started looking further outward — streaming culture, remote work, the particular chaos of a world that changed faster than Springfield's cast ever had to. Some episodes revisited old jokes on purpose, letting longtime viewers feel the callback land. Others introduced entirely new corners of the town, as if Springfield still had more to say about the version of America it had been quietly narrating since 1989. Whatever comes next for the Simpson family, the show has made one thing clear over nearly four decades: Springfield was never really about staying the same. It was about staying recognizable while everything around it kept changing.
